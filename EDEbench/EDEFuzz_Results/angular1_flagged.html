There is a single flow of items. They are put in. They are taken out.<p>In the case of a stream, you put an item in the queue, then it can be removed multiple times by any other process that cares to do so. This may be called &#x27;fan out&#x27;.<p>This is an important distinction and really effects how one designs software that uses these systems. Queues work just fine for, say, background jobs. A user signs up, and you put a task in the &#x27;send_registration_email&#x27; queue.[1]<p>However, what if some _other_ system then cares about user sign ups? Well, you have to add another queue, and the user sign-up code needs to be aware of it. For example, a &#x27;add_user_to_crm&#x27; queue.<p>The result here is that choosing a queue early on leads to a tight-coupling of services down the road.<p>The alternative is to choose streams. In this case, instead of saying what _should_ happen, you say what _did_ happen (past tense). Here you replace &#x27;send_registration_email&#x27; and &#x27;add_user_to_crm&#x27; with a single stream called &#x27;used_registered&#x27;. Each service that cares about this fact is then free to subscribe to that steam and get its own copy of the events (it does so via a &#x27;consumer group&#x27;, or something of a similar name).<p>This results in a more loosely coupled system, where you potentially also have access to an event history should you need it (if you configure your broker to keep the events around).<p>--<p>This is where Postgresql and SQS tend to fall down. In a queue, the idea is once you pop an item off the queue, it&#x27;s no longer in the queue and therefore is gone once it&#x27;s consumed, but with Kafka, you&#x27;re leaving the message where it is and moving an offset instead. This means you can really only have one client per topic, because once the message is consumed, it&#x27;s no longer available to anyone else.<p>There are pros and cons to either mechanism, and there&#x27;s significant overlap in the usage of the two systems, but they are designed to serve different purposes.<p>The analogy I tend to use is that Kafka is like reading a book.
